# Reliability Rubric

Use this as a question set, not a points system. Source history establishes a prior; the particular claim and evidence determine how far belief should move.

## Publisher history

Ask:

- Does the organization publish corrections and preserve updates?
- Does it distinguish news, analysis, opinion, sponsored material, and press releases?
- Does it name methods, documents, data, and uncertainty?
- Does it have demonstrated subject-matter coverage and local access?
- Are there directly documented patterns of fabrication, major correction, propaganda control, or undisclosed conflicts?

Ratings:

- **High:** sustained directly evidenced accuracy/transparency relevant to this work.
- **Medium:** generally credible process with material access or transparency limits.
- **Low:** directly evidenced unreliability, severe undisclosed incentives, or repeated uncorrected falsehoods.
- **Unknown:** not researched or insufficient evidence.

Do not rate by political affinity, national origin, aesthetic professionalism, or reputation inherited from a parent brand.

## Author expertise and track record

Ask:

- Is the byline visible and authentic?
- Is this the author’s established beat?
- Does the publisher biography show relevant role, language, geography, credentials, or prior reporting?
- Does the work itself expose documents, interviews, methods, or domain knowledge?
- Are notable corrections, fabrications, awards, or demonstrated prior accuracy directly documented?
- Does access depend on maintaining a relationship with one faction or institution?

Treat expertise and track record separately. A knowledgeable author can still have a poor accuracy record; an early-career author can produce excellent direct evidence.

Use `unknown` unless the assessment can be tied to retrievable evidence. Employer prestige alone is not author evidence.

## Access

- **Full:** the actual article/document/data was read.
- **Partial:** only part of the item was available; state what was missing.
- **Snippet:** search result or preview only. Supports only its literal text.

## Directness

- **Direct:** raw record, data, imagery, transcript, or firsthand observation available for inspection.
- **Near-direct:** reporter directly interviewed participants, viewed documents, or observed the event, but the underlying evidence is not fully public.
- **Secondary:** summarizes or relies on others’ evidence.
- **Commentary:** interprets known claims without new reporting or primary evidence.

Directness does not imply neutrality. A company launch post is direct evidence of its announcement, not proof of availability or quality. A vendor benchmark is direct evidence of the published run, not proof that the harness, comparison, or workload is fair. A filing is direct evidence of the disclosed metric under its stated accounting definition, not every interpretation placed on it.

## Transparency

Look for:

- named authors and dates;
- visible updates and corrections;
- links to primary material;
- scope and denominator for numbers;
- uncertainty and alternative explanations;
- model assumptions and reproducible methods;
- separation of observed fact from inference.

## Incentives and limitations

Record concrete pressures rather than generic “bias” labels:

- belligerent or stakeholder status;
- political or advocacy mission;
- financial exposure;
- dependence on official access;
- anonymous sourcing;
- censorship or physical access restrictions;
- translation and local-language gaps;
- speed of breaking-news publication;
- data/model assumptions;
- publication’s correction incentives.

## Claim credibility

A claim gains weight from:

- inspectable primary evidence;
- multiple independent evidence paths;
- admissions against interest;
- precise scope, date, and method;
- consistency with other observed facts;
- successful prior predictions made before the outcome;
- survival against serious counter-evidence.

A claim loses weight from:

- circular citation;
- unnamed or unknowable sourcing;
- post-hoc motive stories;
- missing denominator or scope;
- imagery without provenance;
- suspiciously precise inaccessible numbers;
- conflation of capability, intent, and action;
- conflation of announcement, preview, availability, and general rollout;
- benchmark comparisons with different versions, harnesses, hardware, prompts, pricing bases, or exclusions;
- market estimates with incompatible category definitions, geographies, currencies, or periods;
- product recommendations based on stale price or a different regional SKU;
- contradiction by stronger direct evidence;
- language exceeding what the cited evidence entails.

## Probability ranges

Ranges are analyst judgments over hypotheses, not averages of source ratings.

Useful verbal anchors:

- `0.05–0.20`: unlikely but live
- `0.20–0.40`: possible, evidence leans against
- `0.40–0.60`: genuinely unresolved
- `0.60–0.80`: probable, meaningful uncertainty remains
- `0.80–0.95`: strongly supported, residual alternatives remain

Avoid `0` and `1` for empirical modern events. Widen the interval when intent is private, evidence access is poor, reports share sources, or important contradictions remain unresolved.
