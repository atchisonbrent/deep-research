# Event assessment

## Choose this mode when

Something happened or is happening—a conflict, disaster, outage, election, corporate action, scandal—and the question is what actually occurred, why, with what consequences, and what comes next. Use `historical-analysis` when the event closed long enough ago that the record is settled and the question is interpretation; use `security-incident` for breaches and intrusions where technical forensics dominate.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. primary records: official documents, transcripts, filings, court records, imagery with provenance, raw data, first-hand recordings;
2. direct observation and independent field or investigative reporting;
3. timelines reconstructed from dated primary artifacts;
4. stakeholder statements kept strictly in attribution (belligerents, officials, companies, victims, advocates);
5. specialist analysis with inspectable method (OSINT, legal, economic, medical);
6. commentary and social media, as leads and as evidence only of what was said.

## Decomposition pattern

Separate: (a) dated observable events; (b) attributed statements and who benefits from them; (c) casualty, cost, or impact figures with denominator and source method; (d) causal accounts; (e) actor goals versus outcomes; (f) what remains unobserved because of access, censorship, or safety. Write the leading account, the strongest credible alternative, a mixed account, and the insufficient-evidence possibility before retrieving.

## Mode gates

- Every casualty, cost, or scale number carries scope, date, method, and denominator or is marked unknown.
- Anonymous-official reporting is grouped by underlying source path before any corroboration claim.
- Goals-versus-results scoring cites the actor's own stated goals, not a critic's paraphrase.
- Imagery or video carries provenance (original poster, date, geolocation basis) or is treated as unverified.

## Output sections

- Bottom line
- What happened (dated timeline)
- Disputed claims and competing accounts
- Goals and results
- Human and economic cost
- Consequences and second-order effects
- Forecast and update triggers
- Source-confidence map
- Gaps and falsifiers

## Completion criteria

A reader can reconstruct the timeline from primary artifacts, see each contested claim with its competing accounts and independence groups, and identify what evidence would move each hypothesis range.

## Pitfalls

- Treating volume of coverage as corroboration.
- Scoring an actor against goals it never stated.
- Letting a casualty figure travel from a belligerent's press office into the verdict without attribution.
- Assessing motive from incentive alone; incentives are hypotheses, not evidence of decision chains.
- Quietly updating the cutoff as the story moves. Create a dated successor.
