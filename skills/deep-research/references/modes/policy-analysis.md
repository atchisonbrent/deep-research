# Policy analysis

## Choose this mode when

The subject is a public policy, rule, program, or regulation: what it actually says, its implementation state, who it affects, and its likely effects. Use `legal-regulatory` when the question is about legal exposure, compliance obligations, or litigation outcome for a specific actor.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. enacted text, proposed text, regulations, and official guidance, by version;
2. court decisions and administrative rulings interpreting the rule;
3. implementation data: budgets, staffing, enforcement actions, compliance statistics;
4. affected-party evidence with method (surveys, filings, testimony);
5. domain legal and economic analysis with assumptions visible;
6. advocacy and political statements, as attributed positions.

## Decomposition pattern

Separate proposal, enactment, effective date, enforcement, and observed effect. For each provision that matters, record: the rule's text, who it binds, the authority it rests on, its implementation state, and evidence of effect. Write scenarios for effects rather than a single prediction.

## Mode gates

- Every claim about "the rule" cites the version and provision.
- Implementation state is evidenced, not assumed from enactment.
- Effect claims distinguish modelled, observed, and asserted.

## Output sections

- Verdict
- The actual rule (text, version, authority)
- Implementation and enforcement state
- Affected groups and incentives
- Observed and modelled effects
- Legal and operational uncertainty
- Scenarios and triggers
- Gaps

## Completion criteria

Provision-level citations exist for every rule claim; implementation state cites data; effect claims are labeled observed, modelled, or asserted; scenarios name distinguishing evidence.

## Pitfalls

- Analyzing a bill as if it were law, or a law as if it were enforced.
- Adopting an advocate's summary of the text.
- Reporting a modelled effect as an observed one.
