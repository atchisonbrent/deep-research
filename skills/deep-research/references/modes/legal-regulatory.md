# Legal and regulatory

## Choose this mode when

The question is legal exposure, compliance obligation, enforcement risk, or likely outcome of a dispute for a specific actor or class of actors. Use `policy-analysis` for the rule itself and its societal effects.

**Boundary.** This mode produces research, not legal advice. State jurisdiction and date, cite authority, and flag where a licensed practitioner's judgment is required.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. primary law: statutes, regulations, and their official commentary, by version and jurisdiction;
2. binding decisions and orders from courts or regulators in the relevant jurisdiction;
3. enforcement history: actions, settlements, penalties, with dates and facts;
4. official guidance, FAQs, and no-action letters;
5. persuasive authority from other jurisdictions and secondary treatises;
6. law-firm client alerts and commentary, as analysis with commercial incentives.

## Decomposition pattern

For each obligation or exposure: which authority creates it, who it binds, the triggering facts, the consequence, the enforcement record, and the open interpretive questions. Separate what the text says, how it has been applied, and how commentators read it.

## Mode gates

- Jurisdiction and effective date are fixed before any obligation is stated.
- Each exposure cites primary authority; commentary alone does not establish an obligation.
- Enforcement-likelihood claims cite the enforcement record, not the statute's maximum penalty.

## Output sections

- Verdict
- Jurisdiction, actors, and date
- Obligations and exposures (authority-cited)
- Enforcement record
- Open interpretive questions
- Outcome scenarios
- Where professional judgment is required
- Gaps

## Completion criteria

Every obligation traces to primary authority in the named jurisdiction; enforcement claims cite actions; interpretive disputes name the positions; the practitioner-judgment boundary is explicit.

## Pitfalls

- Citing a law-firm alert as the law.
- Ignoring jurisdiction or effective-date differences.
- Reading maximum penalties as expected outcomes.
